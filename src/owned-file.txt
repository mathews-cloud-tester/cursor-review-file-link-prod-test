================================================================================
A History of Compiler Design: Lexing, Parsing, Optimization, and Code Generation
================================================================================

Compilers occupy an unusual place in the history of computing. They are
programs whose sole purpose is to turn other programs into still other
programs, and yet the ideas they embody have shaped almost every layer of
software we now take for granted. Register allocation, static analysis, type
systems, virtual machines, and even large parts of programming-language
semantics grew up inside compiler research before they migrated outward. To
tell the history of compiler design is therefore to tell a history of how
programmers learned to trust machines with the translation of human intent.

That history is often taught as a pipeline: a scanner turns characters into
tokens, a parser turns tokens into trees, an optimizer rewrites those trees
(or some later intermediate form) into something cheaper, and a code generator
emits machine instructions. The pipeline is a useful pedagogical fiction. In
practice the stages have always leaked into one another, and each era of
compiler design is best understood as a period in which one of those stages
became the intellectual center of gravity. This essay follows those centers of
gravity in chronological order: the long struggle to make lexical analysis
mechanical, the golden age of parsing theory, the rise of machine-independent
optimization, and the successive reinventions of code generation from simple
templates to just-in-time compilers and retargetable intermediate
representations.

I. Before compilers: the problem of speaking to the machine
-----------------------------------------------------------

Early electronic computers were programmed in absolute machine code or in
slightly more palatable assembly languages. Grace Hopper, working on the
UNIVAC in the early 1950s, argued that this was a waste of scarce human
attention. If a machine could be made to expand mnemonic macros and to
substitute symbolic names for addresses, then programmers could write in a
notation closer to mathematics and English. The A-0 system and its successors
are sometimes called the first compilers; they were closer to what we would
now call assemblers and linkers with ambitious macro facilities. The
conceptual leap they represent is still the compiler's original promise: that
a program can be a specification of meaning rather than a sequence of
toggle-switch operations.

FORTRAN, begun at IBM under John Backus in 1954 and delivered in 1957, is the
first compiler that a modern designer would recognize as such. It had to
solve every stage of the pipeline at once, with almost no theory to lean on.
The FORTRAN team invented register allocation heuristics, common-subexpression
elimination, and rudimentary control-flow analysis because the generated code
had to rival hand-written assembly or the project would be dismissed as a
curiosity. The success of FORTRAN I is one of the great empirical results in
computer science: it showed that automatic translation could be efficient
enough for production numerical work. Everything that followed was, in one
way or another, an attempt to make that miracle systematic.

ALGOL 60 supplied the other founding document. Where FORTRAN was an
engineering triumph, ALGOL was a linguistic one. Backus-Naur Form (BNF) gave
the community a notation for context-free syntax, nested scopes forced
compilers to think about environments, and the idea of a machine-independent
language made retargetability a first-class concern. The 1960s compilers for
ALGOL, COBOL, Lisp, and a swarm of experimental languages were still largely
hand-crafted, but they established the vocabulary: lexical tokens, syntax
trees, symbol tables, and intermediate code.

II. The lexical analysis era: from ad-hoc scanners to regular languages
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Lexical analysis is the least glamorous stage of a compiler and, for a long
time, the most tediously handwritten. A scanner must recognize identifiers,
keywords, numbers, strings, comments, and a zoo of punctuation, while keeping
track of source locations for later diagnostics. Early compilers implemented
this with large switch statements, character-class tables, and a great deal of
special-case logic for Fortran's column-sensitive layout or COBOL's verbose
noise words.

The theoretical clarification arrived from automata theory. Kleene's regular
events, Rabin and Scott's nondeterministic finite automata, and the subset
construction that turns an NFA into a DFA together implied that a huge class
of lexical patterns could be compiled into a single efficient machine. Ken
Thompson's regular-expression search in QED and, later, grep, showed that the
same theory was useful outside compilers. For compiler writers the decisive
artifact was Lex, built by Mike Lesk and Eric Schmidt at Bell Labs in the
mid-1970s. Lex took a list of regular expressions with associated actions and
generated a DFA-based scanner in C. Flex, by Vern Paxson, later became the
open-source workhorse. The practical lesson was as important as the
theoretical one: lexical analysis could be generated from a declarative
specification, tested independently of the parser, and replaced without
rewriting the rest of the compiler.

Several hard problems kept scanner design from becoming a solved exercise.
Longest-match and rule-priority conventions had to be standardized so that
"if" was a keyword rather than an identifier, and so that "<=" was one token
rather than two. Nested comments, which are not regular, forced either
special start conditions or a retreat to hand-written scanners. Unicode and
international identifiers exploded the character-class tables that 1970s tools
had taken for granted. Modern compilers often still use generated scanners
for the boring bulk of the language and hand-written code for the awkward
corners: raw string literals, nested block comments, Python's off-side rule,
or Fortran's lexical ambiguity between keywords and identifiers.

The deeper legacy of the lexical era is the idea that a compiler front end
can be a composition of formalisms. Regular languages are the right tool for
local, bounded patterns. Context-free grammars, the subject of the next era,
are the right tool for nested structure. The clean cut between the two is an
engineering convenience rather than a theorem about programming languages,
but it is a convenience that has lasted fifty years.

III. The parsing era: grammars, conflicts, and the shape of programs
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Once tokens exist, the compiler must recover phrase structure: expressions,
statements, declarations, and the recursive nestings that make programming
languages more than concatenations of words. The 1960s and 1970s turned this
problem into one of the most complete applied theories in computer science.

Noam Chomsky's hierarchy had already classified grammars. Compiler writers
needed algorithms. Recursive-descent parsing, the oldest practical method,
is simply writing a function per nonterminal and letting the call stack
remember the nesting. It is still the method of choice for many production
front ends because it produces excellent error messages and admits arbitrary
semantic actions. Its weakness is well known: left recursion must be
eliminated, and lookahead must be managed by hand. Pratt parsing and
precedence-climbing parsers later gave recursive descent a principled way to
handle operator hierarchies without a thicket of nonterminals.

Bottom-up parsing offered the opposite trade: the tool would take a grammar
and generate a table-driven machine. Knuth's LR parsing, published in 1965,
showed that a large class of unambiguous grammars could be parsed
deterministically in linear time with a stack and a finite set of states.
The full LR(1) tables were enormous. DeRemer and Penello's LALR(1)
compromise, implemented in Yacc by Steve Johnson in 1975, made the method
practical. Yacc ("Yet Another Compiler-Compiler") and its descendants Bison
and many language-specific clones became the default way to specify syntax
for a generation. A grammar plus precedence declarations plus a handful of
semantic actions was enough to obtain a working parser, a discipline that
fitted perfectly with the Unix philosophy of little languages.

The parsing era was also an era of conflict. Shift/reduce and reduce/reduce
conflicts forced grammar authors to understand their languages more deeply
than recursive descent ever required. The dangling-else problem, the
ambiguity of C's typedef names, and the "lexer hack" needed to parse C and
C++ became folklore. GLR parsing, developed by Tomita and later refined for
programming languages by McPeak, Grimm, and others, offered a way to live
with ambiguity: explore all interpretations in parallel and let later
context, or an explicit tie-breaker, discard the wrong ones. Packrat parsers
and parsing expression grammars (PEGs), popularized by Ford in the 2000s,
took a different route, replacing ambiguity with prioritized choice and
memoization. Each of these techniques is still in production use. GCC's C++
front end is a carefully engineered recursive-descent parser; many language
tools use PEGs; Rust's rustc uses an LL-style parser with dedicated recovery;
some SQL engines still carry Yacc pedigrees.

Error recovery deserves a special mention, because it is where theory most
often surrendered to craft. A compiler that stops at the first syntax error
is a toy. Panic-mode recovery (discard tokens until a synchronizing
delimiter), phrase-level repair, and, more recently, error-tolerant packrat
and GLR techniques all exist to keep the parser moving so that the user sees
more than one diagnostic per run. The quality of recovery is one of the
unheralded differences between a research parser and a compiler people will
tolerate.

By the late 1980s, parsing was widely regarded as a solved problem in the
narrow sense that linear-time algorithms existed for the grammars people
actually wanted to write. The unsolved remainder was linguistic: how to
specify the syntax of C++, of indentation-sensitive languages, of macros that
rewrite syntax before it is parsed, of gradually typed languages whose
surface forms are intentionally forgiving. Those problems did not dethrone
the classic algorithms so much as surround them with preprocessors, name
resolution, and post-parse tree rewrites.

IV. Intermediate representations and the birth of optimization as a field
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Code improvement began as a collection of clever tricks in FORTRAN I and
blossomed into a field when researchers realized that most interesting
rewrites could be expressed on a language-independent intermediate form.
Three-address code, quadruples, triples, and trees all had advocates. The
crucial conceptual move was to separate the front end, which understands a
source language, from the optimizer and back end, which understand a machine
or at least a machine-like intermediate language.

Control-flow graphs, basic blocks, and data-flow analysis, developed through
the 1970s by Frances Allen, John Cocke, Gary Kildall, and many others, gave
optimization a mathematics. Reaching definitions, live variables, available
expressions, and very busy expressions could be computed by iterative
fixpoints on lattices. Kildall's framework made it possible to see a dozen
apparently different passes as instances of one pattern: transfer functions
on a graph, monotonic, and therefore guaranteed to terminate. Allen's work
at IBM, later recognized with a Turing Award, showed that these analyses
could be engineered into production compilers for real architectures.

The 1980s added interprocedural analysis, alias analysis, and a growing
awareness that optimization without precise knowledge of memory was a
guessing game. Fortran's restricted aliasing made aggressive optimization
comparatively honest; C's pointers made it comparatively brave. Dependence
analysis for loops, developed for automatic vectorization and parallelization
at Illinois, Rice, and elsewhere, treated array index expressions as
algebraic objects. The dream of the automatic parallelizing compiler was only
partly fulfilled, but the dependence machinery survives in every serious
autovectorizer.

Static single assignment form (SSA) is the hinge on which modern optimization
turns. Proposed in the late 1980s by Rosen, Wegman, Zadeck, Cytron, and
colleagues, SSA arranges that each variable is assigned exactly once, with
phi functions at control-flow merges. Classical data-flow problems become
simpler; sparse algorithms become natural; a host of rewrites (constant
propagation, global value numbering, dead code elimination, strength
reduction, and more) compose cleanly. Almost every serious compiler since the
late 1990s has used SSA or a close relative. LLVM IR is an SSA form.
GCC's GIMPLE is an SSA form. HotSpot's C2 compiler, JavaScript JITs, and
many research systems adopted the same discipline.

High-level optimizations grew alongside the low-level ones. Inlining,
devirtualization, escape analysis, scalar replacement of aggregates, and
partial evaluation blur the line between language implementation and
optimization. Functional-language compilers contributed continuation-passing
style, closure conversion, and strictness analysis. Object-oriented compilers
contributed class-hierarchy analysis and speculative devirtualization with
deoptimization as a safety net. The optimizer ceased to be a machine-code
polisher and became a place where language semantics are exploited.

V. The code generation era: from templates to the allocation of scarcity
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Code generation is the art of making finite machines do infinite-looking
work. Early generators were templates: walk the tree, emit a naive
instruction sequence, hope the optimizer had already done enough. That
approach collapses as soon as the machine has a small register file, delay
slots, or a zoo of addressing modes.

Register allocation became the emblematic back-end problem. Graph coloring,
introduced to compilers by Chaitin and colleagues at IBM, models live ranges
as vertices and interference as edges; a k-coloring is an assignment to k
registers. Spilling, coalescing, and live-range splitting turned the elegant
NP-complete picture into a family of heuristics that still dominate teaching
and, in various forms, production. Linear-scan allocation, popularized by
Poletto and Sarkar and later refined in JIT compilers, traded optimality for
speed, which is the right trade when compilation itself is on the user's
critical path. SSA-based allocation, as in the work of Hack, Paleczny, and
the LLVM community, brought the two eras together.

Instruction selection is the other classic back-end problem: how to cover an
intermediate tree or DAG with machine instructions of unequal power. Maximal
munch, dynamic programming over trees (as in BURS and iburg), and, later,
DAG combiners and machine-description files all tried to make retargeting a
matter of writing a specification rather than a new compiler. GCC's RTL and
machine-description macros, the lcc code generator of Fraser and Hanson, and
LLVM's SelectionDAG and GlobalISel are successive answers to the same
question. VLIW and EPIC architectures briefly made instruction scheduling and
bundling as important as selection; out-of-order superscalar machines then
moved some of that burden back into hardware, without ever making software
scheduling irrelevant.

The reduced-instruction-set movement of the 1980s changed the economics of
code generation. If the hardware exposed a simple load-store machine, the
compiler's job became more important, not less: delay slots, exposed
pipelines, and an insistence that compilers, not microcode, should implement
high-level operations. Patterson, Hennessy, and the compilers that accompanied
MIPS, SPARC, and later ARM made this argument concrete. The later return of
CISC in the form of x86, with micro-op translation inside the processor, did
not restore the old template generators; it produced hybrids in which the
compiler still thinks in RISC-like three-address code while the processor
thinks in fused operations and cache lines.

Just-in-time compilation reopened every back-end question under a latency
budget. Smalltalk, Self, and the Deutsch-Schiffman dynamic translation work
showed that runtime type feedback could beat static guesses. Java's HotSpot,
JavaScript engines such as V8, SpiderMonkey, and JavaScriptCore, and the
tracing JITs used in some Lua and JavaScript implementations all treat code
generation as a speculative, layered activity: interpret first, compile
cheaply, compile better, and be prepared to deoptimize when speculation
fails. Register allocation must be fast. Instruction selection must be good
enough. The interesting design problem is not a single perfect instruction
sequence but a policy for spending compile time.

VI. Retargetability, virtual machines, and the modern stack
-----------------------------------------------------------

A persistent dream of the optimization and code-generation eras was a
compiler that could be retargeted by writing a machine description rather
than a new program. UNCOL in the 1950s was an early, unsuccessful attempt at
a universal intermediate language. P-code for Pascal, the JVM, and the .NET
CIL succeeded by narrowing the dream: a virtual machine for a family of
languages, not for all languages and all machines. GCC showed that a single
optimizer could serve many source languages and many processors if the
intermediate representation was carefully chosen and the community was large
enough to bear the cost of machine descriptions. LLVM, begun by Chris Lattner
and Vikram Adve, made the same argument with a cleaner SSA IR, a library
architecture, and a license that encouraged adoption in industry. Clang,
Rustc's LLVM back end, Swift, Julia, and a long list of research languages
turned LLVM into the default code-generation platform of the 2010s.

MLIR, emerging from Google and the LLVM community in the late 2010s,
generalized the lesson again. Different abstraction levels want different
intermediate languages: tensor operations for machine learning, loops for
scientific codes, CFG-plus-SSA for scalar optimization, and machine
instructions for emission. A compiler is no longer one IR with a few
lowerings; it is a stack of dialects with explicit conversions. That is an
old idea (GCC's GENERIC, GIMPLE, and RTL already formed a stack) restated
with better tooling for extensible IRs.

WebAssembly added a new code-generation target that is neither a physical
machine nor a language-specific VM. Its linear memory, structured control
flow, and evolving SIMD and garbage-collection extensions have forced
compilers to treat the browser (and the Wasm runtime outside the browser) as
a first-class back end. Source maps, debug information, and the tension
between compact download size and optimized execution recapitulate, in a new
key, the old FORTRAN requirement that generated code be good enough to
replace the thing people used before.

VII. What the four eras leave us
--------------------------------

Lexical analysis taught us that local structure can be generated from regular
formalisms and that the remaining irregularities of real languages are a
small, if annoying, residual. Parsing taught us that nested structure has a
rich algorithmic theory and that the practical problem is often error
recovery, ambiguity, and the interaction with name resolution rather than the
existence of an algorithm. Optimization taught us that most of a compiler's
value after correctness is a collection of rewrites on a well-chosen
intermediate form, and that SSA is the most successful such form yet found.
Code generation taught us that machines are exercises in scarcity (registers,
issue width, cache, compile time) and that the back end is where language
independence goes to die unless the IR and the machine descriptions are
engineered with unusual care.

The pipeline metaphor survives because it is a good way to assign
responsibilities. It fails as history because each era left residue in the
others. Scanners consult symbol tables in C. Parsers perform limited type
analysis to resolve ambiguities. Optimizers invent new IRs that look like
source-level constructs. Code generators re-do peephole optimization that an
earlier pass could have done. Production compilers are archaeological sites:
a generated lexer here, a hand-written Pratt parser there, an SSA optimizer
in the middle, a table-driven instruction selector at the bottom, and a JIT
policy wrapped around the whole thing.

If there is a fifth era, it is already underway, and it is not a new stage
in the pipeline so much as a new way of filling the old stages. Machine
learning has been used to predict inlining, to allocate registers, to
schedule instructions, and to search superoptimizer spaces. Language models
now write code that compilers must accept, which quietly raises the value of
diagnostics and of undefined-behavior sanitizers. Hardware has become a zoo
of vector units, matrix accelerators, and explicitly managed memories, which
pulls domain-specific IRs and scheduling into the center of compiler design
the way RISC once pulled instruction selection there.

The FORTRAN team's original bet remains the right one. Programmers will write
in notations that fit their problems. Machines will execute instruction sets
that fit their transistors. Compilers will stand in between, and the quality
of that standing-in-between will continue to depend on four crafts that
matured in different decades: how we recognize words, how we recover
structure, how we rewrite meaning into cheaper meaning, and how we assign
that meaning to the finite resources of a machine. The tools change. The
division of labor has proven remarkably stable. That stability is the real
inheritance of compiler design, and it is why a file that exists only to be
linked from a review comment can still be a reasonable place to remember how
the craft was built.
